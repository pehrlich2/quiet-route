Risks

What could go wrong

A one-truck company has one of everything, so every failure stops the route. Most of these risks are cheap to cover if we plan for them before launch. The one we can't fully control is access to the dump.

What if the truck is totaled?

  • What insurance pays: commercial auto with physical damage coverage pays the truck's actual cash value (market value at the time, not what we paid) minus the deductible. A 2024 Silverado EV loses value fast, so if we financed it, gap coverage pays the difference between that value and the loan.
  • Add two coverages: rental reimbursement (pays for a replacement vehicle, usually capped at a daily amount and number of days) and business income (covers lost revenue while we can't operate). Ask the broker for quotes on both. They're cheap compared with a month without service.
  • Replacement: a comparable used Silverado EV costs ~$55–60k and takes 2–6 weeks to find, buy and register.
  • Backup tow vehicle, in order of preference:
    1. An agreement with a local contractor or hauler who owns a ¾-ton pickup with a brake controller and 7-pin plug. Pay a day rate. Almost any heavy-duty pickup can pull the trailer.
    2. A commercial truck rental. Enterprise Truck Rental rents heavy-duty pickups for towing. Confirm the nearest location and that their terms allow our trailer and trash. Many consumer rentals, including U-Haul, restrict towing trailers that aren't theirs and commercial use.
    3. Our own used gas ¾-ton pickup (~$15–25k), once we're past ~500 customers. It doubles as the bulky-pickup truck.

What if the trailer breaks?

A dump trailer is simple. The likely failures are cheap: flat tires, wheel bearings, the 12 V pump, the dump battery, a hose, or wiring. The expensive failures are rare: a bent frame from a crash, or a cracked hoist.

  • Keep on hand: a mounted spare tire, a spare hydraulic pump motor, battery and charger, a hose, a 7-pin plug, bearings, and a hitch pin (~$600–1,000 total).
  • A second trailer is cheap insurance once past ~600 customers. A used 7×12 is ~$6–8k. It's also useful for bulky-item days and spring cleanups, and it can be the "compost and recycling" trailer. The tipper is the custom part. Mount it with bolts so it can move between trailers.
  • Before that, borrow or rent. Equipment rental shops rent dump trailers but usually prohibit trash, so ask. Better: arrange with a landscaper to borrow their dump trailer. It's the same trailer we use.
  • The tipper is the piece that would really stop us (no carts can be lifted). Keep a spare solenoid and pump. If it fails, lifting by hand for a day is slow but possible.

Snow and scheduling

  • Follow the industry norm and slide a day. If we can't run, every remaining route that week moves one day later. A Saturday make-up day catches the spillover, so no one waits two weeks. Announce it with the same notifications as weather delays.
  • Early on, the schedule has plenty of slack. At 300 customers, route days are 2–3 hours. Even at 1,200 customers they're 5–7 hours. There's room to fold a missed day into the next few without overtime.
  • Later, budget 5–10% overtime for December through March once route days fill up, plus the Saturday.
  • Set clear customer rules: carts at the end of the driveway on a plowed surface, not in the snowbank. The notification the night before can remind them when snow is in the forecast.

When the driver calls in sick

A hired driver runs the routes, and the founder has a day job, so there's no one on staff to step in. That makes a trained backup driver essential from launch: named on the insurance, paid a day rate, and having ridden each route at least twice. The planner's stop lists and the driver app make routes learnable. A second backup covers the case where both are out.

A mutual-aid pool of small haulers

We found no staffing service built for small haulers. General gig apps (Instawork, Wonolo, GigSmart, Bacon Work) focus on warehouses and delivery in large metros, not rural Vermont with a trailer. So the idea is for several small haulers and compost companies to share a roster of relief drivers. Each driver is approved in advance on each company's insurance, trained on a couple of routes, and paid a set day rate by whichever company calls. The companies could also lend each other a truck or trailer. Farmers already share labor and equipment this way. Lake Champlain Sanitation / No Waste Compost, Myers and other independents are natural partners, and it positions us as the friendly independent rather than a threat.

Could Casella play dirty?

Probably not in dramatic ways. A small hauler isn't worth the legal risk. But ordinary competition can hurt:

  • Dump pricing: a higher rate for small private haulers, or a per-load minimum that penalizes our ~1.5–2-ton loads. Protection: a written rate agreement with a term, and a priced-out alternative transfer station.
  • Targeted discounts on the streets where we sign people up. Protection: founding-member price locks, community or co-op ownership, and service they can't copy (the live truck, compost, the quiet).
  • Contract lock-in: auto-renewing contracts with cancellation fees, mostly for businesses. Protection: easy-exit terms as a selling point, and helping customers understand when their current contract can end.
  • Hiring away our driver with benefits. Protection: pay fairly, go home when the route is done, profit sharing.

Predatory pricing and denying rivals access to the only dump could raise antitrust and state concerns, but they're hard and slow to prove. The written dump agreement is the real protection.
