Business plan · Chittenden County, Vermont · September 2026

Quiet Route Hauling

Quiet Route is a battery-electric trash, recycling and compost service for Jericho, Essex, Hinesburg and Richmond. It launches with a used electric pickup pulling a dump trailer, which costs about a third as much as a garbage truck. The pitch to customers is the same pickup, without the diesel noise at 6 a.m. It works if routes are dense, the Williston dump rate is fair, and equipment stays cheap until the customer base is there.

Figures are planning estimates from public sources and our own route modeling. Quotes come before commitments.

The short answer

Start with a pickup and a trailer

A used Silverado EV, a dump trailer, a cart tipper and a tarp come to about $85–95k all in. A small electric garbage truck runs $200k+. The setup also needs no commercial license (CDL), which widens the hiring pool.

Break-even is about 200 customers

That's with a hired driver paid per route day, recycling included, and a $50/month average price.

One truck serves ~550–800 customers

Both carts are collected on the same day. The top of the range needs a midday fast charge in winter. After that we add a split trailer or a second truck.

Everything tips in Williston

Trash, recycling and food scraps each go to a facility in Williston, within 3 miles of each other. Dump runs fit easily inside a day's charge.

Price by cart size

Vermont requires household trash to be priced by volume or weight, so we offer 32, 64 and 96 gallon tiers, with recycling included.

Get the dump rate in writing first

Casella owns the Williston transfer station and the landfill it feeds, and it's also the largest hauler we'd compete with. We get a written rate before buying anything.

Daily operationTwo passes a day, dump runs, charging

Route days

Each customer has one pickup day every other week, with both carts out. The driver makes two passes over that day's houses: one for recycling, one for trash.

At ~100 stops a day that's ~50 houses a day. 400 customers ≈ 4 route days a week. A full schedule of ~500–550 customers fills all 10 days in the two-week cycle.

Loads and hours

Each pass fills the trailer about once or twice. Trash goes to Casella's transfer station, and recycling to the CSWD recycling facility 300 m away.

Run recycling first. The recycling facility closes at 3pm and the transfer station at 4pm. Each Williston run adds ~16 miles and ~40 minutes.

Charging

A 48 A Level 2 charger at the yard refills a normal day overnight. The longest winter days need a midday fast charge. Richmond's ChargePoint by I-89 Exit 11 has two 125 kW stalls. We charge right after a dump run, with the trailer empty, at roughly $15–20 a stop.

Recommendation: sign up homes one neighborhood at a time, and add businesses on the same streets that use carts. A stop 100 feet from the last one costs almost nothing. One two miles away costs real money. Dumpsters wait until a second truck makes sense.

Pricing

  • Check the $50 draft against what others charge. Get quotes from Casella, Myers and the small local haulers for weekly and every-other-week service. If we're above their weekly price, we sell quiet electric pickup as a premium service.
  • Price by cart size to meet Vermont's volume-pricing rule. For example, every other week: 32 gal $35, 64 gal $50, 96 gal $65 per month. Weekly at about 1.7× those prices.
  • Bill in advance on autopay, monthly or quarterly, by card or bank transfer. Chasing unpaid invoices from hundreds of small accounts eats margin.
  • Charge extra for overflow bags, big items, and make-up pickups after holidays.
  • Carts stay company property (sourcing and cost on the Hardware page). We recover them with a one-time ~$25 cart fee, or waive it for a 12-month commitment.

Billing businesses

  • Price by cart size × pickups per week, as a flat monthly rate. Add fees for extra pickups and for contaminated recycling.
  • Use a one-year agreement that renews automatically but can be cancelled with 30 days' notice. Being easy to leave is a selling point against the big haulers' contracts.
  • We skip the fuel surcharge that diesel haulers add, and advertise that.
  • Use hauler software (CurbWaste, TrashFlow, Routeware) for billing and routes.

RecyclingRequired by Vermont law, and the biggest factor in how many customers a truck can serve

Vermont's Universal Recycling Law requires any hauler collecting household trash to offer recycling collection, with its cost built into the trash price (no separate line item). None of our four towns collects recycling itself, so it's on us.

Leading option: both carts on the same day

Customers put both carts out on one day every other week. The truck makes a recycling pass, dumps, then makes a trash pass. It's the simplest arrangement for customers: one day to remember, and nothing left at the curb for a second day. The cost is that two passes land on the same day, so the longest winter days need a midday charge to reach full capacity.

Fallback: alternate weeks

Trash one week, recycling the next, same weekday. It spreads the work across more days, so it needs midday charging less often. It's common in New England, but it's more to remember.

Future plan: a split trailer

A lengthwise divider lets one pass collect both carts. Myers already does this locally with split-body trucks. The route planner shows it serves ~25–35% more customers per truck. Design on the Hardware page.

CompostingFood scraps have been banned from Vermont landfills since 2020, so customers will ask

Where the buckets ride

Not on trash days. The truck bed is empty on trash days, but the RST's ~1,300–1,500 lb payload is mostly used by the ~900 lb trailer tongue. That leaves room for only ~10–15 full buckets. Instead, run compost as its own route without the trailer. With the trailer off, the full payload is available: ~35–40 buckets on a bed rack, and much better range. One compost day a week covers ~150–200 bucket customers on every-other-week service.

Where it goes

Green Mountain Compost in Williston, run by the county solid waste district (CSWD). It's a different facility from Casella's transfer station, but in the same town, so a combined run is short. The hauler fee per ton isn't published, so call them (802-660-4949). They fine haulers for contamination: $75/ton when a load is 5–10% contaminated and $150/ton over 10%. Compostable liners and one warning tag before refusing a bucket keep us under that.

Price

Bootstrap charges about $16 per every-other-week visit in the Boston area. Here, $12–18/month as an add-on for every-other-week service seems right (estimate; tested in the founding campaign). Lake Champlain Sanitation / No Waste Compost already collects food scraps in our towns. Talk to them: they could be a partner or a subcontractor for compost before we're a competitor.

Team and hiringA hired driver from day one

Roles: the founder keeps a full-time job in software and runs the business side: the software, billing, sales, accounting and dump-site accounts. A hired driver runs the routes from launch, with a trained backup driver for sick days and vacations.

What the job is like: trash collection is consistently among the five or six deadliest jobs in the US, mostly from traffic at the curb and from backing accidents. It's also physical, early-morning work in all weather. People stay for being home every night, finishing when the route is done, and steady work.

  • It starts part-time. 200–300 customers is 2–3 route days a week. It becomes a full-time job around ~450–550 customers.
  • No commercial license needed for the pickup and trailer, so far more people qualify.
  • Offer a four-day week, or pay by the route, so the driver goes home when it's done.
  • The tipper keeps people. No lifting means fewer back injuries, lower workers' comp and less turnover.
  • Pay ~$24–30/hr (estimate; check local postings). Without benefits we'd lose people to Casella and town public works.
  • Where to find people: construction dump-truck drivers who want winter work, school bus drivers who want the summer covered, and retirees for backup shifts.

Growth pathWhat comes after the first truck fills up

  1. Split trailer (~$3–7k on top of a second trailer). One visit collects both carts, for ~25–35% more customers per truck. The first trailer becomes the backup.
  2. Second pickup and trailer at ~550–800 customers. It adds capacity and covers breakdowns.
  3. A compacting truck at ~1,000+ customers: a Class 6 electric chassis rated at 25,995 lb, which still needs no commercial license, with a 10–13 yd compactor (~$300k).

Fundraising and marketingA founding-member campaign, run on our own site

The pitch is "help us bring quiet, electric trash pickup to your street," not "buy our service." Density is the whole business, so every neighbor who signs up makes the route cheaper for everyone. That gives customers a real reason to recruit their neighbors.

Why build our own instead of using Kickstarter

  • Every pledge becomes a customer record in our billing system, with an address checked against our route zones, a cart size and a saved payment method. Kickstarter backers would have to be re-signed-up later, and some would drop off.
  • We keep the 5–10% platform fee.
  • We can show live progress per zone, such as "Jericho Wk B: 23 of 30 neighbors." That's more motivating than one national total.
  • We can copy Kickstarter's all-or-nothing idea: save the card now, charge only when the zone hits its threshold. Stripe supports saving a card without charging it.

Rules we have to follow: prepaid money isn't ours until we deliver service. Keep it in a separate account, publish a launch date, and refund automatically if a zone doesn't launch by that date. Vermont's consumer protection law applies. Keep it simple and honest.

Bigger money: debt, community equity, or a co-op

  • Equipment loan (SBA 7(a), VEDA, or dealer financing). This is the normal route for the ~$85–95k setup.
  • Vermont community investment. Vermont's Small Business Offering exemption lets a Vermont company raise money from Vermont residents. The limit is at least $1M; sources differ on whether it's since been raised. Ordinary investors can put in up to $10,000 each. Customers who become owners make strong word-of-mouth marketers.
  • A co-op is more workable than it seems. Grocery co-ops also hire specialized staff: the members own the business and professionals run it. A consumer co-op (customers own it) or a multi-stakeholder co-op (workers and customers own it together) fits "we're in it together." Examples in this field: Pedal People in Northampton (a worker co-op hauling trash by bicycle for 10–12% of the town's households) and CERO in Boston (a worker co-op collecting commercial compost). The Vermont Employee Ownership Center and the Cooperative Fund of the Northeast help co-ops get started. The cost is slower decisions and more paperwork. It's worth one conversation with VEOC before we set up the company.

Marketing that fits a density business

  • Front Porch Forum posts, one town at a time, linking to that zone's progress bar.
  • Street captains: the first signup on a road gets a free month for every 3 neighbors who join.
  • Ongoing referral credit: $10 off for both sides.
  • The quiet electric truck is the ad. Put a large "Quiet Route" wrap and the website address on the trailer.

What the company is worthRules of thumb, not an appraisal

Small hauling companies usually sell in one of two ways. Buyers either pay about 1–1.5× annual revenue for the customer accounts (the "route"), or a multiple of profit before interest, taxes, depreciation and owner pay. For small, owner-run haulers that multiple is roughly 3–6× (estimate). Casella is actively buying: six acquisitions in the first half of 2025 and five in the first half of 2026. So there's a real buyer if we ever want one.

Open questionsWhat we still need to find out

  1. Casella's rate for a small private hauler, and whether there's a minimum charge per load. That matters at ~1.5–2 tons a load.
  2. Where the truck and trailer park overnight. Zoning has to allow it, it needs a 240 V circuit, and a trailer that smells in August needs some distance from neighbors.
  3. Same day or alternate weeks? The founding campaign asks customers directly.

First 90 days

  1. Weeks 1–2: prices and rules. Casella's Williston rate and any per-load minimum, in writing. CSWD's recycling fee and hauler-license requirements. Green Mountain Compost's hauler packet. ANR on cart-size pricing and what counts as "offering" compost and yard waste. Competitors' prices.
  2. Weeks 2–6: founding campaign. A sign-up page that checks each address against our zones and shows each zone's progress, promoted on Front Porch Forum in two towns first. Every sign-up answers two questions: same day or alternate weeks? and how do you get rid of trash today? Target: 150 reserved, 50 prepaid.
  3. Weeks 3–6: equipment. Shortlist used Silverado EVs (window sticker, payload, battery report, four-wheel steering, wheel size). Trailer quotes. A tipper fabrication quote that includes the mid-length mount, the deflector, and bracing for a future divider.
  4. Weeks 4–8: company and money. Form the company (after the co-op conversation). Financing, insurance quotes, a yard with 240 V, permission at the Richmond charger. Try two or three GPS trackers.
  5. Weeks 6–10: hire and train. A driver and a backup. Dry runs of real routes from the planner, including a full dump cycle and a midday charge.
  6. Weeks 8–12: launch. USDOT number, state hauler permit, CSWD license, registration. Billing live, carts delivered. Start with the zones at threshold, 1–2 route days a week, and weigh every load to calibrate the planner.

What the numbers leave out

  • For diesel: more payload (~2,500–3,500 lb) and towing (13–18k lb), no range limit, and no midday charging to plan around.
  • For electric: quiet (the brand), no idling fumes at customers' houses, bed outlets that run the tipper, and simpler upkeep.
